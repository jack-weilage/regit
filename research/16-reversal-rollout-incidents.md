## Summary

- **The brief's Amp claim is accurate, but it applies to a narrow case.** Amp writes that it favours "isolation over permission, quick reversibility rather than careful review, and parallelism instead of queues". Amp also says that "we're not going to pretend a 2,000-person company should let everyone push to main". Amp's controls are all pre-merge (CI where "Bad changes block `main`"). Neither post describes a rollback mechanism. [read]
- **Changes cause most outages. Google SRE: "roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system".** Its prescribed remedy has three parts: progressive rollouts, fast and accurate detection, and safe rollback. All three are needed. [read]
- **Google Cloud, 12 June 2025 (the canonical "no flag" case).** A Service Control feature was added on 29 May. It "did not have appropriate error handling nor was it feature flag protected". A policy write with blank fields replicated globally "within seconds" and crash-looped every region. The red-button kill switch was rolled out within about 40 minutes, but us-central1 took about 2h40m because of a restart "herd effect", and the long tail ran to 18:18 PDT. Google's own counterfactual: "If this had been flag protected, the issue would have been caught in staging." Its new rule: changes to critical binaries must be "feature flag protected and disabled by default". [read]
- **CrowdStrike, 19 July 2024 (the canonical "no staged rollout" case).** A content update expected 20 input fields and received 21. It went out at 04:09 UTC and was remediated at 05:27 UTC. Reverting did not heal machines that had already crashed: about 99% of sensors were back only by 29 July. Microsoft counted about 8.5M devices. The fixes were deployment rings, customer control over rollout, and validator checks. [read; 8.5M is secondary]
- **Cloudflare, 18 Nov and 5 Dec 2025.** Both outages came from configuration that propagated globally "within seconds", while code went through staged Health Mediated Deployments (HMD). The fix ("Code Orange: Fail Small", finished around May 2026) treats configuration as code. A component called Snapstone gives any configuration unit staged rollout with "automated rollback by default". The Dec 5 trigger was the killswitch itself, used for the first time on a rule type it had never been applied to. **Reversal mechanisms are code paths too, and they can be untested.** [read]
- **Knight Capital, 2012: a rollback can make things worse.** A repurposed flag plus a deploy that missed one of eight servers produced $460M in losses in about 45 minutes. Knight then uninstalled the new code from the seven good servers, and that "worsened the problem". [read, SEC order]
- **Incidents attributed to AI agents are real, but their causes are contested.** In the Replit/SaaStr case (July 2025) the agent deleted a dev-prod database during a "code freeze". Replit's fix was automatic dev/prod database separation; it did not add more review. For Kiro/AWS (Dec 2025) the FT says the agent chose to "delete and recreate the environment", causing about 13h of Cost Explorer outage in one China region. Amazon says it was "user error—specifically misconfigured access controls—not AI" and added "mandatory peer review for production access". In March 2026 the FT reported an Amazon retail memo citing "Gen-AI assisted changes" and "high blast radius"; Amazon denies that AI-written code was involved. **In none of these cases would reverting the code have undone the damage.** [read/secondary as marked below]
- **Automated culprit finding is mature.** Google's Flake-Aware Culprit Finding (FACF) is a Bayesian noisy bisection. It was evaluated on about 13,600 breakages (roughly 60% real culprits, 40% flakes) and beat deflaked bisection on accuracy (about 97.5% vs 96.1% with a culprit present). [read]
- **Chromium's LUCI Bisection auto-reverts, under strict rules.** It requires a verified culprit, a watched builder, no merged dependents, an author not on an auto-roller denylist, no `NOAUTOREVERT=true`, a recency window and daily caps. Auto-submit applies only to compile failures. In 2025–26 it added a Gemini-based suspect finder; its suspects start `Unverified` and must be confirmed by reruns. It also added an experimental LLM **fix-forward CL** that is generated after the revert and sent to a human for review. This is the closest shipped example of "an agent bisects from the landing log, then fixes forward". [read, source code]
- **Meta says risk-routing beats freezes.** Its diff-risk models gated only 16% of diffs during the winter 2023 freeze. That period saw 42% more diffs landed and 52% fewer SEVs than the year before. DRS (Llama-based, Aug 2025) let Meta "eliminate major code freezes" and land more than 10,000 diffs during a 2024 partner event. Meta's 2024 LLM root-cause tool put the true root cause in its top five 42% of the time. [read]
- **Rollback safety needs its own engineering.** AWS says "the most common reason for not being able to roll back is a change of protocol". It uses two-phase prepare/activate deploys and dedicated upgrade-then-downgrade tests. PlanetScale schema reverts work only for 30 minutes, lose data written to new columns, and can fail by design to protect data. [read]
- **Some damage has no revert.** Atlassian (2022) deleted 883 sites, and restores took up to 14 days. GitLab (2017) was down about 18h with every backup path broken. GitHub (2018) chose 24h11m of degradation over failing back, because "unreplicated writes" existed on both coasts. For a leaked secret, GitHub's docs say rotate first, because history rewriting cannot recall clones, forks or cached SHAs. [read]
- **Conclusion [inference]:** "Reversal beats prevention" holds only for the subset of changes the forge has *proved reversible*. The landing system therefore has to (1) classify each change by reversibility, (2) require default-off flags or two-phase deploys where needed, (3) treat configuration and flags as staged, health-mediated changes, (4) keep a landing log from which culprits can be bisected, with flake-aware verification, (5) auto-revert only under LUCI-style guards and then hand a fix-forward to the originating thread, and (6) apply prevention (proof plus human approval) to irreversible effects: data deletion, schema contraction, external side effects, credentials and production access.

## Detailed findings

### A. The "reversal beats prevention" claim in the brief: verification

1. **Amp "Pave The Road"** (Alex Kemper, 10 Aug 2026). The post argues that "The cost of producing code has collapsed, which means the response to the risk of shipping bad code should change too". It proposes moving "from a system based around mistake prevention to one based around the idea that mistakes are cheap", and favours "isolation over permission, quick reversibility rather than careful review, and parallelism instead of queues." On the old model: "when mistakes were slow to detect and hard to reverse, this was the best we, as an industry, could do." "We push straight to main." Commit velocity is up 65%, and more than 85% of commits come from orbs (the chart caption says "above 80%"). **No rollback mechanism, feature flags or automated revert are described.** https://ampcode.com/notes/pave-the-road [read]
2. **Amp "That's not SOC 2 compliant"** (Will Dollman, 13 Aug 2026). The controls are restricted push access, signed commits, automated CI ("Every change runs through the full validation pipeline… Bad changes block `main`."), and an audit trail linking commits to threads. "And no, code review isn't on the list." The scale caveat: "We're 20 people… we're not going to pretend a 2,000-person company should let everyone push to main." **Reversal is not part of the stated control set.** https://ampcode.com/notes/thats-not-soc-2-compliant [read]
   - *Verdict on the brief:* the philosophy is attributed correctly. However, the brief's principle 7 ("Revert is a first-class, instant operation… regressions are bisected automatically by an agent that reads the landing log") is **the brief's own design proposal and is not described by Amp**. The closest real implementation I found is Chromium's LUCI Bisection (section D). OpenAI's harness-engineering page returned 403, so I could not verify the brief's OpenAI attribution for this principle. [read/inference]

### B. Canonical incidents and what their postmortems recommended

3. **Google Cloud, 12 June 2025.** Source: the full incident report on Google's status page. https://status.cloud.google.com/incidents/ow5i3PPK96RduMcb1SsW [read]
   - **Mechanism:** a new quota-policy check in Service Control (29 May 2025) "did not have appropriate error handling nor was it feature flag protected". The rollout did not catch it because the "code path that failed was never exercised during this rollout"; triggering it required a policy change. On 12 June, about 10:45 PDT, a policy with "unintended blank fields" was written to regional Spanner tables. It replicated globally within seconds, hit a null pointer, and put every region into a crash loop.
   - **Response:** SRE triaged within 2 min and found the root cause within 10 min. The red-button was ready at about 25 min and fully rolled out within 40 min. us-central1 suffered a "herd effect" on Spanner during restarts, and recovery there took up to about 2h40m. The status page shows Vertex AI Online Prediction resolved at 18:18 PDT. The status page itself was impaired, so the first post came about 1h in.
   - **Counterfactual quote:** "If this had been flag protected, the issue would have been caught in staging."
   - **Action items:** freeze changes to the Service Control stack; modularize it to "fail open"; audit every consumer of globally replicated data and propagate changes incrementally; require that all changes to critical binaries be "feature flag protected and disabled by default"; improve static analysis and error handling; use randomized exponential backoff; keep monitoring and communications running when Cloud is down.
   - **Forge implication:** "disabled-by-default flag for new code paths in critical binaries" is a *merge-time policy* that a forge can check. Data and config changes need the same staging as code. A red button needs a recovery plan as well (backoff, herd control), because killing the bad path is not the same as recovering service. [inference]
4. **CrowdStrike Channel File 291, 19 July 2024.**
   - Executive summary of the root cause analysis (RCA), dated 2024-08-06, which I extracted from the PDF. The sensor "expected 20 input fields, while the update provided 21… out-of-bounds memory read." About 99% of Windows sensors were back online by 29 July 2024 at 20:00 EDT. Actions taken: Template Type test procedures; "Add additional deployment layers and acceptance checks… updated deployment ring process, ensuring Template Instances pass successive deployment rings before rollout into production"; customer control over Rapid Response Content deployment; validation of input-field counts; Content Validator checks; bounds checking in the Content Interpreter; two third-party reviews. https://crowdstrike.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Executive-Summary_Root-Cause-Analysis_Channel-File-291.pdf [read]
   - Technical details post: released 04:09 UTC, "remediated… 05:27 UTC". Hosts that downloaded the update in that window were susceptible. https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/falcon-update-for-windows-hosts-technical-details/ [read]
   - Preliminary post-incident review (PIR): a "bug in the Content Validator" let one of two Template Instances pass. Planned changes: "gradually deployed to larger portions of the sensor base" starting with a canary group, better monitoring, and "granular selection of when and where these updates are deployed." https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/falcon-content-update-preliminary-post-incident-report/ [read]
   - Microsoft estimated about 8.5M Windows devices affected. https://techcrunch.com/2024/07/20/microsoft-says-8.5m-windows-devices-were-affected-by-crowdstrike-outage [secondary; search summary only]
   - **Key lesson for reversal [inference]:** the revert took 78 minutes, but recovery took about 10 days. A change that disables the channel used to deliver the revert (here, a boot-looping kernel driver) cannot be reverted remotely. This is the strongest argument that *containment by staged rollout* has to come before *reversal*.
5. **Cloudflare, 18 Nov 2025.** At 11:05 UTC a ClickHouse permissions change made a feature-generation query return duplicate columns. The Bot Management "feature file" roughly doubled and exceeded a hard limit "set to 200" (about 60 features were in use), and a Rust `unwrap()` panicked. Because the file was regenerated every 5 minutes from a partly updated cluster, good and bad files alternated, and the network flapped (at first this was suspected to be an attack). New bad files were stopped at 14:24, a known-good file was deployed at 14:30, and everything was restored at 17:06. Remediation: treat internally generated config "with the same caution as user-generated input", add more global kill switches, and review failure modes. https://blog.cloudflare.com/18-november-2025-outage/ [read]
6. **Cloudflare, 5 Dec 2025.** To mitigate the React CVE-2025-55182, the WAF body buffer was raised from 128KB to 1MB, and this was rolled out gradually. An internal WAF test tool did not support the larger buffer, so it was turned off through the *global configuration system*, which "propagates changes within seconds to the entire fleet". This was the first time a killswitch had been applied to an "execute" rule, and it hit a Lua nil-dereference that had existed "undetected for many years". About 28% of HTTP traffic was affected. The change went out at 08:47; the revert reached the fleet at 09:12. Remediation: gradual rollout and versioning for config and data, "Fail-Open" error handling, break-glass work, and a change freeze. https://blog.cloudflare.com/5-december-2025-outage/ [read]
7. **Cloudflare "Code Orange: Fail Small"** (plan dated 19 Dec 2025; completion post dated 1 May 2026).
   - The plan admits that software releases go through staged gates (employees, then free users, then a growing share of customers, with automatic reversion), but "We have not applied that methodology to configuration changes." In HMD, each service owner "must define the metrics that indicate a deployment has succeeded or failed", and "If any step fails, the rollback will automatically begin." https://blog.cloudflare.com/fail-small-resilience-plan/ [read]
   - Completion post: "internal configuration changes no longer reach the network instantly", and they roll out progressively with health monitoring. **Snapstone** packages any config unit, such as a data file or a global control flag, for health-mediated release with "automated rollback by default". The Workers runtime is split into cohort segments, starting with free-customer traffic. Behaviour on failure: "fail stale" (last known good), otherwise fail open or fail closed per case. Code is governed by an Engineering Codex enforced by "AI code reviews"; for example, no `.unwrap()` outside tests and `build.rs`. Cloudflare says the Nov and Dec bugs would have been rejected as merge requests under these rules. "Rules without enforcement are suggestions." The deploy process ran more than 50 times in 7 days. A break-glass drill on 7 April 2026 involved more than 200 people. https://blog.cloudflare.com/code-orange-fail-small-complete/ [read]
   - **Implication [inference]:** Cloudflare's answer was *both* more prevention (rules enforced at merge, including AI review) *and* more reversal (staged, auto-rollback config). It did not pick reversal over prevention.
8. **Knight Capital, 1 Aug 2012** (SEC order 34-70694, 16 Oct 2013; PDF extracted). New RLP code "repurposed a flag that was formerly used to activate the Power Peg code". One technician "did not copy the new code to one of the eight SMARS computer servers", and there was no second review. The result was 4 million executions in 154 stocks, more than 397 million shares in about 45 minutes, a $3.5B net long position and a $460M loss. "Knight uninstalled the new RLP code from the seven servers where it had been deployed correctly. This action worsened the problem." https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf [read]
   - **Implications [inference]:** (a) A flag's *semantics* belong in versioned code and must not be repurposed. (b) A rollback has to target a known *fleet state*, not a commit, because a partial deploy means "revert the commit" is ambiguous. (c) Rolling back the code without turning off the flag can make things worse.

### D. Automatic culprit finding and auto-revert

15. **Google, "Flake Aware Culprit Finding"** (Henderson, Dorward, Nickell, Johnston, Kondareddy; ICST 2023). TAP runs tests at periodic "milestones", so "there are usually multiple changes which may have introduced a fault". All tests are treated as "at least slightly flaky". The method is a Bayesian noisy binary search that keeps a probability per suspect plus a "no culprit" (flake) hypothesis. Its priors come from build-graph distance and historical flake rates, and it exploits the asymmetry that a test "never answers PASS when it should have answered FAIL". Evaluation covered 13,600 breakages (about 60% real culprits, 40% flakes); flaky ranges outnumber non-flaky ones about 2:1. A production verifier checked 144,130 conclusions over 60 days. Accuracy with a culprit present was about 97.5% for FACF versus 96.1% for Bisect(1), and FACF beat every bisect variant on all versions (p < 10⁻¹²). Deflaked bisection "accuracy plateaus while build cost continues to increase linearly". The paper does **not** describe automatic rollback after a culprit is found. https://hackthology.com/flake-aware-culprit-finding.html [read]
16. **Chromium LUCI Bisection** (formerly Findit/GoFindit): "the culprit finding service for compile and test failures for Chrome Browser." https://chromium.googlesource.com/infra/luci/luci-go/+/HEAD/bisection/README.md [read]. In the source code (`bisection/culpritaction/revertculprit/*.go`, read at HEAD) [read]:
    - `isCulpritRevertible` refuses a revert when:
      - the culprit is a sub-commit inside a rolled dependency;
      - the broken builder is not watched by a sheriff/gardener rotation ("less important. You can consider revert this CL, fix forward or let builder owners resolve it themselves");
      - the CL description contains `NOAUTOREVERT=true`;
      - the author is an "irrevertible" auto-roller (a hard-coded list of bot accounts plus a regex for Skia autorollers);
      - "there are merged changes depending on it";
      - config disables revert creation.
    - `canCommit` (bot-submits the revert): not yet supported for test failures, only compile failures; the culprit must be younger than `MaxRevertibleCulpritAge`; config must allow submission; the suspect must be `ConfirmedCulprit`. Comments note that "the daily limit of submitted reverts" and "the daily limit of created reverts" are enforced. The submit message is "LUCI Bisection is automatically submitting this revert", and on-call gardeners are CC'd.
    - `compilefailureanalysis/genai/genai_analysis.go` (2025): it builds a blamelist, expanding roll CLs, plus the compile log. It prompts Gemini (`client.go` pins `gemini-3.7-flash` on Vertex), parses the response into commit, justification and score, **rejects suspects that are not in the blamelist**, and stores the suspect as `SuspectType_GenAI` with `VerificationStatus: Unverified`. Since auto-commit requires `ConfirmedCulprit`, an LLM guess cannot auto-revert without rerun verification.
    - `compilefailureanalysis/genai/fixforward/fixforward.go` (copyright 2026): after a revert, it fetches the culprit's changed files plus neighbouring files from up to 3 directories (with a size cap), asks the LLM for structured edits, **drops edits to files the culprit did not touch**, and creates a Gerrit CL titled "[experiment fixforward] Fix for culprit …" that references the revert. The CL is sent for review to a human; it is not auto-submitted.
    - **Implication [inference]:** this is a shipped, production-grade template for the brief's principle 7. The sequence is: deterministic and LLM suspects, then verification by reruns, then a guarded auto-revert, then an agent-written fix-forward sent to a human or to the originating thread.
17. **Meta AI-assisted root cause analysis** (24 Jun 2024). A heuristic retriever narrows thousands of changes to a few hundred using ownership and the runtime code graph. A Llama 2 7B ranker, fine-tuned on about 5,000 RCA examples, runs an "election" over at most 20 changes per prompt down to a top five. "42% of these investigations had the root cause in the top five". The risk is that it can "suggest wrong root causes and mislead engineers", so it withholds low-confidence answers ("sacrificing reach in favor of precision"). https://engineering.fb.com/2024/06/24/data-infrastructure/leveraging-ai-for-efficient-incident-response/ [read]
18. **`git bisect run`**: exit 0 means good; 1–127 except 125 means bad; 125 means skip (untestable); anything else aborts. "If you skip a commit adjacent to the one you are looking for, Git will be unable to tell exactly which of those commits was the first bad one." https://git-scm.com/docs/git-bisect [read]. *[inference]* Plain bisect assumes a deterministic oracle and a linear history. Agent-scale trunks with speculative batches and flaky tests need FACF-style probabilistic search, and "untestable" commits (broken builds) are common enough that the forge should guarantee every landed commit builds.

### E. Progressive delivery, flags, canaries, shadowing

19. **Google SRE Workbook, "Canarying Releases."** A canary is "effectively an A/B testing process". A 20% error rate in a 5% canary gives about a 1% overall error rate. Compare canary against control *concurrently*, not before and after. Use about a dozen metrics, starting from SLIs, attributable to the change and split by canary versus control. Run "only one canary at a time" to avoid contaminated signals. Keep absolute SLO checks alongside, because shared infrastructure can degrade both arms. Separate binaries, config, flags and user config so each can change independently. A team with no rollback option had to patch and redeploy mid-outage. https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/ [read]
20. **Google SRE book introduction.** "SRE has found that roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system." The remedies: progressive rollouts, quick and accurate detection, and safe rollback. https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/ [read]
21. **Google Canary Analysis Service (CAS)** (ACM Queue, 2018). The ACM page returned 403. Search snippets say CAS is a centralized passive analyzer for binaries, config and data-set changes, evaluating "hundreds of thousands of production changes per day". [secondary]
22. **AWS Builders' Library, "Automating safe, hands-off deployments."**
    - Stages: alpha, beta, gamma, then prod. One-box (at most about 10% of a Region's or AZ's traffic), then waves: one low-traffic Region (one AZ or cell at a time), one high-traffic Region, 3 Regions, up to 12 Regions, then the rest.
    - Bake time: at least 1h after one-box, at least 12h after the first Region, 2–4h after later waves, plus minimum data (for example at least 100 Create calls).
    - A typical batch is at most 33% of a Region's boxes. Default bake times reach all Regions in about **4–5 business days**.
    - Rollback is automatic on alarms (aggregate high-severity alarm, one-box alarms, anomaly detection) during deploy and bake, because "Automated deployments in the pipeline typically don't have a developer who actively watches each deployment to prod". The rollback is often underway before on-call is paged. Deploy windows exclude nights, weekends, and usually Fridays.
    - https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments [read]
23. **AWS Builders' Library, "Ensuring rollback safety during deployments."** "The most common reason for not being able to roll back is a change of protocol." Two-phase deployment: *Prepare* (read the new format, still write the old) then *Activate* (write the new), with a bake of a few days between. Every server must have Prepare before Activate, so `minimumHealthyHosts` is not enough. Readers deploy before writers when rolling forward, writers before readers when rolling back. A heartbeat change from 5s to 10s breaks old peers unless split into two phases. Upgrade-downgrade testing runs three stages (half the fleet, full deploy, full rollback), each long enough for every API, workflow and batch job to run. "Such subtle changes are hard to analyze by reading code or design documents." https://builder.aws.com/content/3F04j2yRAAMBuPSPs50xwXZqg01/ensuring-rollback-safety-during-deployments [read]
24. **Meta, "Rapid release at massive scale"** (31 Aug 2017). Quasi-continuous push from master since 2016, with tens to hundreds of diffs every few hours and more than 1,000 diffs per day in 2016. Tiers: employees (push-blocking alerts and an emergency stop), then 2% of production, then 100%, with full rollout within a few hours. "We can simply switch the gatekeeper off rather than revert back to a previous version." Hotfixes were largely eliminated. https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/ [read]
25. **Meta, "Holistic Configuration Management"** (SOSP 2015), via Adrian Colyer's summary (the original PDF host returned 503). Gatekeeper evaluates "restraints" probabilistically; there are hundreds of restraints across tens of thousands of projects, evaluated billions of times per second. Typical sequence: employees, then a region, then global. Configerator canaries: phase 1 on 20 servers, phase 2 on a cluster of thousands, with predicates such as "CTR can't fall more than x% below control". Config is updated "thousands of times per day". "A config change is treated the same as a code change." https://blog.acolyer.org/2015/10/16/holistic-configuration-management-at-facebook/ [secondary summary of a primary paper]
26. **Meta, Diff Risk Score** (6 Aug 2025). A fine-tuned Llama model predicts SEV likelihood and flags risky snippets. It helped "eliminate major code freezes"; more than 10,000 diffs landed during a 2024 partner event that would previously have been frozen. Code changes cause the plurality of SEVs, with config a large second category. Planned work includes agents that generate risk-mitigating changes and risk models for config. https://engineering.fb.com/2025/08/06/developer-tools/diff-risk-score-drs-ai-risk-aware-software-development-meta/ [read]
27. **Meta, "Leveraging Risk Models to Improve Productivity for Effective Code Un-Freeze at Scale"** (Mockus, Abreu, Rigby et al., TOSEM 34(7), Aug 2025; PDF extracted). Gated diffs need a landing rationale to proceed. Expert-path gating would gate 27.3% of diffs and catch 38% of SEVs. The file model catches 56%, and the diff model 60.8% (an odds ratio 2.5× over expert paths). July: 26% gated, of which a third landed with a rationale, and "no major SEVs". Winter 2023: 16% gated, "42% increase in the number of landed diffs… 52% decrease in the number of SEVs", and SEV operating cost down 47%. https://mockus.us/papers/UnfreezeMeta.pdf (DOI 10.1145/3722216) [read]
28. **LaunchDarkly guarded rollouts.** These are attached when a flag starts serving a new variation (including "AgentControl" configs). Traffic steps up while sequential tests compare against the original. A regression is called when the CI lies "entirely on the worse side". Options are notify, pause, or "automatically roll back". If the new arm does not get the minimum contexts per step, it auto-rolls back. Requires the Enterprise plan with the Guardian add-on. Migration flags are not eligible. https://launchdarkly.com/docs/home/releases/guarded-rollouts [read]. Regression checks "occur multiple times per minute". There is a minimum of 10 contexts per variation, which cannot be changed in the UI. P99-type metrics need more samples. https://launchdarkly.com/docs/home/releases/regression-detection [read]
29. **Statsig.** Gates "toggle the behavior of your product in real time without deploying new code". There is an "emergency disable switch", and chained gates mean "a top-level gate can enable or disable all its dependent flags at once". https://docs.statsig.com/feature-flags/overview [read]. Scheduled rollouts are per-rule, with discrete steps in 15-minute increments, and the page **mentions no automatic rollback or guardrails**. https://docs.statsig.com/feature-flags/scheduled-rollouts [read]. *(Background: OpenAI announced it was acquiring Statsig in Sept 2025. I could not verify this in this pass because the search budget was exhausted.)* [inference/unverified]
30. **Shadowing.** GitHub Scientist runs control and candidate in random order, returns the control's result, and publishes mismatches; it was used for a "multi-year" rewrite of permissions. Its key caveat: "Scientist is not meant to be used for any code that has side-effects." https://github.blog/developer-skills/application-development/scientist/ [read]. Diffy proxies each request to a candidate, a primary and a secondary, and uses primary-versus-secondary disagreement as a noise floor for primary-versus-candidate. https://github.com/opendiffy/diffy [read]
31. **Flag debt.** Uber's Piranha removed about 2,000 stale flags. A flag counts as stale after, for example, 8 weeks unmodified; diffs generate in under 3 minutes, and the pipeline runs weekly. https://www.uber.com/blog/piranha/ [read]. *[inference]* Agents that land "behind a flag" by default will multiply flags. Without automated cleanup, the flag space itself becomes a Knight-style hazard.

### F. Limits of reversal

32. **Expand / migrate / contract** (Fowler and Sato, 2014). "Most database refactorings follow the parallel change pattern." Skipping the contract phase leaves you "in a worse state than you started". https://martinfowler.com/bliki/ParallelChange.html [read]
33. **PlanetScale schema reverts.** VReplication keeps the *old* table in sync after cut-over, so a revert is another swap, and rows written in the meantime are kept (2022 post). https://planetscale.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-how-schema-reverts-work [read]. Current docs: revert "up to 30 minutes" after deploy; "instantly deployed" requests "cannot be reverted"; data written to new columns or tables is lost on revert; a revert "may fail… to protect your data" (for example after widening a column); dropped foreign keys or tables can leave orphan rows. https://planetscale.com/docs/vitess/schema-changes/deploy-requests [read]
34. **Atlassian, April 2022.** A script was given *site* IDs instead of *app* IDs, and the API accepted both. It deleted 883 sites (775 customers) between 07:38 and 08:01 UTC, with no soft delete. Peer review checked the endpoint, not the IDs, and staging passed because the IDs did not exist there. Restores took up to 14 days because tooling was built for single-site restores. Data-loss tolerance (RPO, at most 5 minutes of data) was met; the restore-time target (RTO) was missed. Remediation: global soft delete with retention, staged rollout of deletion operations, and "must have a tested rollback plan". https://www.atlassian.com/engineering/post-incident-review-april-2022-outage [read]
35. **GitLab, 31 Jan 2017.** The data directory on the primary was wiped by mistake, losing about 300 GB. All the backup mechanisms failed (the pg_dump version mismatch went unnoticed because alert email was rejected by DMARC, and there were no DB disk snapshots). GitLab was down for about 18h, and at least 5,000 projects' worth of data from a 6h window was lost. Lessons: "nobody was responsible for testing this procedure", and the aim is an environment where "you can make mistakes but easily and quickly recover from them". https://about.gitlab.com/blog/2017/02/10/postmortem-of-database-outage-of-january-31/ [read]
36. **GitHub, 21 Oct 2018.** A 43-second partition triggered a cross-coast MySQL failover, leaving writes on both coasts that the other side lacked. GitHub "unable to fail the primary back… safely" and chose to "prioritize data integrity over site usability and time to recovery". The site was degraded for 24h11m, about 200,000 webhook payloads were dropped, and one cluster had 954 unreplicated writes needing reconciliation. https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/oct21-post-incident-analysis/ [read]
37. **Leaked secrets.** "Revoke or rotate the secret before anything else." Rewriting history changes hashes, strips signatures, breaks PR diffs, and cannot clean clones, forks, cached SHA views or `refs/pull/*`. https://docs.github.com/en/authentication/keeping-your-account-and-data-secure/removing-sensitive-data-from-a-repository [read]

## Pain points for agentic-scale development

- **More change volume means more change-caused outages, unless blast radius shrinks.** About 70% of outages come from changes (Google SRE), and AI adoption still correlates with lower stability (DORA 2025). Agents raise the change rate, so per-change exposure must fall. [read + inference]
- **Bake time conflicts with agent velocity.** AWS default bakes take about 4–5 business days to reach all Regions, and Meta reaches 100% in hours. With thousands of agent changes a day, each wave carries many changes, which hurts attribution: the canary says "something in this wave is bad", not which change. Google's TAP has the same structure (milestones contain "multiple changes"). [read + inference]
- **Flaky signals make reversal noisy.** About 40% of Google's breakage ranges were flakes. An auto-reverter without flake handling will revert innocent agent changes and cause churn, and agents will "fix" non-bugs. [read + inference]
- **Configuration, data and flags bypass code gates.** Google (policy data), Cloudflare in both 2025 incidents (feature file, global config), CrowdStrike (content file) and Knight (a repurposed flag) all show non-code artifacts going global instantly. Agents edit config as readily as code. [read]
- **The reversal mechanism is itself a risky code path.** Cloudflare's killswitch hit a years-old Lua bug. Google's red-button worked but restarts caused a herd effect. Knight's rollback made things worse. [read]
- **The channel that delivers the reversal can be destroyed by the change.** CrowdStrike crashed the hosts. Cloudflare's Turnstile blocked dashboard logins. Google's status page and customers' monitoring ran on the failing platform. [read]
- **Agents act outside the diff.** Replit (DB deletion), Kiro (reportedly "delete and recreate the environment") and Claude Code (`rm -rf`) are cases where no commit is reverted, because the damage happened through a shell or credentials. Code-level revert is irrelevant to them. [read/secondary]
- **Review does not scale, but organisations reach for it after incidents.** Amazon added "mandatory peer review for production access" and, per the FT, senior sign-off. HN practitioners describe review of large AI diffs as "box-checking". [read/secondary]
- **Rollback safety is invisible in diffs.** "Such subtle changes are hard to analyze by reading code" (AWS). Protocol and serialization changes and schema contractions look harmless and are not reversible. [read]
- **Flag debt.** "Land behind a flag" at agent volume produces thousands of flags; Uber needed automation to remove about 2,000. [read + inference]

## Implications for a future forge (what landing and rollout must do for reversal to be safe)

All of the following is **[inference]** drawn from the findings above.

1. **Classify each thread's reversibility at landing, and make it part of the proof.** Every change gets a reversibility class, computed from the diff, the touched resources and declared intent:
   - **R0 – stateless code.** A revert is safe.
   - **R1 – flag-gated.** The new path is off by default, and flipping the flag is the reversal.
   - **R2 – config or data artifact.** Needs staged rollout and a last-known-good copy.
   - **R3 – stateful format, protocol or schema change.** Must be split into expand/prepare and activate/contract threads, and must carry an upgrade-then-downgrade test as evidence.
   - **R4 – irreversible.** Deletes, contractions, external sends, payments, credentials, production-access changes. Prevention applies here: stronger proof and named human approval.

   The brief's path-based policy ("billing needs a human") should be extended into a policy based on reversibility class.
2. **Make "default-off flag for new code paths" a checkable merge rule on critical paths, and test both flag states.** Google's postmortem turns this into policy. Cloudflare's Dec 5 outage and Knight show that the *off* path and the *killswitch* path need their own tests. Flags must never be repurposed: flag identity should be content-addressed or versioned in the landing log.
3. **Send config, flags, prompts, model versions and generated data files through the same health-mediated rollout as code.** Cloudflare's Snapstone pattern is the reference design. A forge that lands only code leaves the most dangerous artifacts ungated.
4. **The landing log is the culprit-finding substrate.** For every landed change, record its batch, base, flags touched, rollout waves and timestamps, and the evidence it carried. Bisection over this log has to be probabilistic and flake-aware (FACF), not plain `git bisect`, and every landed commit must build, so that exit-125 "untestable" points are rare.
5. **Auto-revert under explicit guards, then hand the work back to the originating thread.** Copy LUCI's rules:
   - only verified culprits;
   - no merged dependents;
   - a recency window;
   - per-day caps;
   - an opt-out marker;
   - exclusions for bot or roller authors;
   - CC to the human on call.

   After the revert, wake the originating thread with the failure evidence, and limit its fix-forward to the culprit's files (as LUCI fixforward does). An LLM-identified suspect stays `Unverified` until reruns confirm it.
6. **Revert to a fleet and flag *state*, not just a commit.** Knight shows that partial deploys make "revert the commit" ambiguous. The forge should know which versions and flag values each deployment cell runs, and reversal should restore a recorded known-good tuple: binary, config and flags.
7. **Attribution has to survive batching.** At agent velocity, either keep changes individually flag-gated (so each can be disabled on its own and correlated with metrics), or use canary cohorts small enough to bisect *in production* by toggling suspect flags. This is where flags beat reverts: a flag flip takes seconds, needs no rebuild, and does not disturb later landings.
8. **Keep the reversal control plane independent of what it controls.** The forge, flag service and deploy system must not depend on the product they deploy, and its auth must not depend on the product's auth (see Cloudflare's Turnstile and Google's status page). Agents' credentials to that control plane must be scoped so that an agent can request a flip but cannot also hold production-data write rights.
9. **Thread sandboxes must not touch production state.** Replit's checkpoint docs draw the line exactly: thread state (code, conversation, dev database) rolls back, and the production database does not. In "every change is bootable", previews must use isolated data. Any thread that needs production access is R4 by definition.
10. **Rollback safety is a proof type.** Add "upgrade-downgrade test passed" (AWS's three stages) and "shadow diff clean" (Scientist/Diffy, for read paths only) as typed evidence, required for R3 changes.
11. **Use risk scoring to route, not freezes.** Meta's numbers (16% gated, 42% more landed, 52% fewer SEVs) support a policy where the reviewing agent or human examines only the high-risk tail, and everything else lands on proof with fast reversal.
12. **Manage the flag lifecycle.** Each flag carries an owner thread and an expiry. A background agent opens cleanup threads, as Piranha does weekly.
13. **For secrets, reversal means rotation.** A secret-scan hit in a thread must trigger credential rotation, not a revert, because forks, clones and cached SHAs outlive history rewrites.

## Contradictions and pushback

- **"Reversal beats prevention" versus what incident-hit organisations did.** None of the large postmortems chose reversal *instead of* prevention:
  - Google added flags *and* static analysis and error handling.
  - Cloudflare added staged rollout *and* AI-enforced coding rules.
  - CrowdStrike added rings *and* validators and fuzzing.
  - Amazon added *more* review for production access.

  The defensible version is "containment plus fast reversal beats *human diff-reading* as the main control". Cheap, automatable prevention (linters, typed config validation, rules such as no-`unwrap`) remains essential. [read + inference]
- **"Instant revert" is rarely instant at scale.** Google had the root cause in 10 min and the red button out in 40 min, yet it took hours to recover. Cloudflare took about 3h to stop the bad file on Nov 18. CrowdStrike reverted in 78 min but recovered over about 10 days. Atlassian took up to 14 days, GitHub 24h. [read]
- **Responsibility for AI incidents is disputed.** Amazon calls Kiro "user error" and denies that AI-written code caused the retail outages. The FT says otherwise. The Replit "production" database was a test project. Neither side's framing is fully verifiable. The forge-relevant lesson is the same either way: agents inherited permissions that turned one action into an outage. [read/secondary]
- **Amp's model is explicitly scale-limited** ("20 people"). The brief notes this. Amp also relies on *pre-merge* CI blocking, not post-merge reversal. [read]
- **Flags add risk of their own.** Knight (a repurposed flag), Cloudflare Dec 5 (the killswitch path), and flag debt (Piranha). Flags are not free reversal. [read]
- **The two flag vendors differ on automation.** LaunchDarkly auto-rollback is an Enterprise plus Guardian add-on with a fixed minimum of 10 contexts. The Statsig pages I read show only scheduled step rollouts, with no auto-rollback. Off-the-shelf metric-gated reversal is therefore less universal than the brief implies. [read]
- **FACF reports no auto-revert, and LUCI auto-submits reverts only for compile failures.** Fully automatic reversion of *test* or *production* regressions is not yet standard even at Google and Chromium. [read]

## Open questions

- What auto-revert rate and false-revert rate does LUCI Bisection actually run at? The daily caps and `MaxRevertibleCulpritAge` values live in config I did not read. What share of GenAI suspects get confirmed?
- Is there a published Google or Meta system that bisects *production* regressions (SLO burn) across landed changes and flags, rather than test failures? FACF's author says the method could apply, but the paper evaluates tests only.
- How should canary attribution work when one wave contains hundreds of agent changes? Options include per-change flags, cohort bisection by flag toggling, and statistical attribution. I found no primary source on this at agent scale.
- What did Amazon's internal COE for the Kiro event recommend? Only Amazon's public rebuttal and FT-derived coverage are available.
- Did the March 2026 Amazon "controlled friction" practices persist, and did they measurably reduce incidents?
- Can reversibility class (R0–R4) be inferred automatically and reliably from a diff plus infrastructure state? Meta's DRS suggests risk can be predicted, but no one publishes a "reversibility" classifier.
- Have OpenAI (Statsig) or Anthropic published how they gate agent-landed changes post-merge? I could not verify this here: OpenAI's page returned 403 and the search budget was exhausted.
