# 24: Big Tech internal forges and AI in review and landing, as precedent for "policy over evidence"

Researcher key: `24-bigtech-ai-review-risk`. Compiled 2026-10-12.

## Summary

1. **Google's rule is still that a human approves every change, and the share of AI-written code keeps rising.** Pichai wrote on 2026-04-22: "Today, 75% of all new code at Google is now AI-generated and approved by engineers, up from 50% last fall." [read] On 2025-10-09 the figure was "nearly half ... generated by AI, reviewed and accepted by engineers." [read] The Oct 2024 figure, "more than a quarter," comes from the earnings call. [secondary] Each statement says a human approved the code. None claims that approval was automated.
2. **Google's AI in Critique is mostly on the author's side.** ML-suggested edits resolve reviewer comments. The model is calibrated to 50% precision, it fires on about 50% of eligible comments, and authors apply 40–50% of the suggestions they preview. In June 2024 this added up to ">8% of review comments ... addressed with AI-based assistance." [read] AutoCommenter, an LLM best-practice commenter, reached 54% to 66% "useful" ratings and roughly a 40% comment-resolution rate. Its A/B test showed no significant change in review duration. [read]
3. **Google has run "policy over evidence" for LSCs for more than ten years, without AI.** Rosie splits a large-scale change (LSC) into shards along OWNERS boundaries, tests each shard on TAP, and routes all of them to a single **global approver**. That approver uses pattern-matching tools and "need[s] to manually examine only a small subset that are anomalous." Rosie can "create, test, review, and submit thousands of changes per day." LSCs are 10–20% of changes in a project. [read] This is the clearest precedent: a human approval still exists, but it covers a *class* of change, not each diff.
4. **Google's LLM migrations kept human review.** The 2025 paper states: "Humans review the LLM-generated code the same way as any other code" and "Code reviews and change rollouts still require a human operator." In the int32→int64 migration, 80% of the code in landed CLs was fully AI-authored. In JUnit3→4, about 87% of the AI code was committed unchanged. **Review speed was the bottleneck.** [read]
5. **Meta's Diff Risk Score is a real production example of routing by risk.** It began as a logistic-regression DiffModel. During the winter 2023 freeze, only 16% of diffs were gated, and 5.7% of all diffs still landed after the developer gave a "landing rationale." Meta landed 42% more diffs than the previous year, SEVs fell 52%, and SEV operating cost fell 47%. [read] A gated diff "will be blocked from landing" unless the engineer waits, de-risks it, or escalates it to a manager. [read]
6. **The LLM version of DRS is better offline but was not shown in production.** A risk-aligned iDiffLlama-13B, gating the top 5/10/50% of diffs, caught 26.2/42.3/88.5% of SEVs. Logistic regression caught 18.7/27.9/84.6%. The paper describes only the logistic regression as deployed ("for more than 9 months"). [read] Meta's 2025 blog says DRS is now a "fine-tuned Llama" that let "10,000+ code changes" land during a 2024 partner event, but gives no accuracy numbers. [read]
7. **Meta found that showing AI patches to reviewers slowed review down.** In MetaMateCR's first safety A/B test, reviewers who saw AI fix patches spent 5.5% more time in review (p=.029). Meta rolled it back and then showed the patches only to authors, after which no regression appeared. [read] This is a direct data point: **AI output put in front of a reviewer adds review load.**
8. **Meta's JiTTests produce evidence for each diff and ask for a human only when a test fails.** LLM-generated catching tests run when a change is submitted. Rule-based and LLM assessors "reduce human review load by 70%." Of 41 candidate catches, 8 were confirmed bugs and 4 were serious. [read] Meta's own description: tests "only require human review when a bug is actually caught." [read]
9. **TestGen-LLM established the pattern of filtering by evidence before a human sees anything.** A generated test is proposed only if it builds, passes all five runs, and raises coverage. Engineers accepted 73% of the survivors. [read]
10. **Microsoft runs an AI reviewer on more than 90% of PRs,** which is ">600K pull requests per month." It "does not commit [suggested changes] directly." The author has to click apply. On 5,000 onboarded repos, median PR completion time improved 10–20%. [read] Nadella's "20%, 30% of the code ... written by software" (LlamaCon, 2025-04-29) is [secondary].
11. **Uber's uReview analyzes more than 90% of about 65k diffs per week** (the source also says "per month" in one place). 75% of rated comments are marked useful and 65% of posted comments are addressed. Human-written comments are addressed only 51% of the time. Uber estimates savings of about 1,500 hours per week. It built a grader stage to filter comments and dropped categories such as style. [read] AI review here is an extra reviewer, not an approver.
12. **Amazon moved toward more human approval after AI-linked outages.** The FT and Ars Technica reported in March 2026 that junior and mid-level engineers would need senior sign-off on AI-assisted changes. Separately, there was a 90-day "code safety reset" with "controlled friction" on about 335 Tier-1 retail systems. [secondary] Amazon disputed that senior approval is required for all AI-assisted changes and said "only one of the recent incidents involved AI tools in any way." [secondary, quoting Amazon] Amazon's own post blames an AWS Cost Explorer event on a "misconfigured role" and added "mandatory peer review for production access." [read]
13. **Amazon's Q transformation (2024)** reported that "our developers shipped 79% of the auto-generated code reviews without any additional changes," along with 4,500 developer-years saved and more than 50% of production Java systems upgraded in under six months. [read, via Simon Willison's quote of Jassy's post]
14. **The pattern across all of them:** AI shows up as (a) an author-side helper, (b) an extra reviewer that comments, (c) a risk classifier that gates landing, and (d) a generator of verified changes for a narrow class, such as tests or migrations. **No Big Tech company has publicly said it removed the human approval for general changes.** Where approval has been reduced, as with Rosie global approvers, DRS low-risk landing during freezes, and JiTTests reviewing only on a catch, the reduction is scoped to a change class or a risk band, and an escape hatch with a recorded rationale remains. [inference]

## Detailed findings

### A. Google

#### A1. Approval model in Critique / Piper (pre-AI baseline)
- Three roles: LGTM (correctness and comprehension), **OWNERS** approval ("Owners act as gatekeepers for their particular directories"), and **readability** (language style and best practices, from a company-wide pool). "Most reviews have one person assuming all three roles." A small change is about 200 lines, about 35% of changes touch a single file, and most reviews have exactly one reviewer. Feedback is expected within 24 working hours. https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch09.html [read]
- Critique scoring: a change can be committed with at least one LGTM, enough approvals, and no unresolved comments. "LGTM and Approval are hard requirements and can be granted only by reviewers." "Every change requires an LGTM regardless of approval status." Analyzer findings appear as chips, and a reviewer can turn one into a blocking comment with "Please fix." A presubmit failure "blocks the change from being sent for review or committed." https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch19.html [read]
- Forge implication [inference]: Google already splits approval into separate predicates (correctness, ownership, style) that one human can satisfy together. That is policy-as-code over typed approvals. A future forge can treat each predicate as something a human, a checker, or an agent could satisfy, decided per path.

#### A2. Rosie and large-scale changes (the strongest non-AI precedent)
- Rosie "shards it based upon project boundaries and ownership rules." Each shard is tested, mailed, and submitted independently. It caps the number of outstanding shards, runs at low priority, disables some per-project precommit checks, and ignores presubmit failures that predate the change. An owners-detection service picks reviewers and adds new ones when an owner does not respond. Rosie can "create, test, review, and submit thousands of changes per day." https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch22.html [read]
- Governance: a committee of about a dozen people approves LSC proposals. Reviews are routed to a **global approver** because "it's cheaper to have a single expert understand the nature of the change" and automate the review. Global approvers use pattern-matching tools and "need to manually examine only a small subset that are anomalous." Local owners are added only when "their review is required for context." The book is candid that owners often give LSCs only cursory review because "they trust the engineers generating LSCs too much." [read]
- TAP train for LSCs: a train starts every three hours. Each change first runs a random sample of 1,000 tests. The union of affected tests then runs, which can take more than six hours. Failing tests that are not flaky are rerun per change. "Most shards affect fewer than one thousand tests." Recently flaky tests are ignored during automated submission. [read]
- Numbers: peak throughput was more than 700 changes and 15,000 files per day during the `scoped_ptr` migration, and sometimes ten times that now. LSCs are 10–20% of changes in a project. Tooling beats manual editing above about 500 edits. One LSC series removed more than a billion lines in three days. [read]
- Forge implication [inference]: this is the clearest evidence that **approval by change class** works at very large scale. One accountable human approves the generator and the pattern, and machines check that each shard matches the pattern. Only anomalies go to humans. Agent swarms making many similar edits fit this model directly.

#### A3. ML-suggested edits to resolve review comments (2023)
- The model was trained in the DIDACT style on Google's monorepo history of comment → author edit. It was calibrated "for a 50% target precision." Offline it addresses 52% of comments at that precision. Online, suggestions exceed the confidence threshold for about 50% of eligible comments. **40–50% of previewed suggestions are applied by authors.** The preview rate rose from about 20% to about 40% after a "Show ML-edit" button was added. More than 70% of applications happen in the code review tool. The fraction of comments addressed by ML edits doubled from beta to launch. Time savings of "hundreds of thousands of hours annually" are *projected*, not measured. Suggestions go to **authors**. Showing them to reviewers while they draft comments is listed as future work. https://research.google/blog/resolving-code-review-comments-with-ml/ (2023-05-23) [read]

#### A4. AI in SWE at Google, June 2024 status
- Code completion acceptance rate is 37%. AI assists "the completion of 50% of code characters," where the denominator is typed plus accepted characters and paste is excluded. ">8% of review comments are now addressed with AI-based assistance." Smart paste accounts for about 2% of IDE code. Authors "increasingly become a reviewer" of AI suggestions. https://research.google/blog/ai-in-software-engineering-at-google-progress-and-the-path-ahead/ (2024-06-06) [read]
- Note [inference]: the June 2024 "50% of characters" is a different metric from the 2025–26 CEO "% of new code" numbers. They should not be combined into one trend line.

#### A5. AutoCommenter (LLM best-practice reviewer)
- T5-based. It posts comments in the code review tool on a differently colored background and as IDE diagnostics. Comments link to a best-practice document. Thresholds are set per URL; an earlier global threshold was t=0.98. Comments on unchanged lines are suppressed. The developer-rated useful ratio plateaued at about 54% and reached 66% after suppression, against an 80% target. The rater-study useful ratio was 60% and then 74%. Comment resolution is estimated at about 40%. It covers 68% of historical human best-practice comments that cite a URL. In the A/B test, roughly half of developers took part, and there was **no statistically significant change in review duration, active review time, or iterations**. Deployed to tens of thousands of developers. The motivation is the slow, monotonous work of readability mentors. https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13565 [read]
- Forge implication [inference]: the readability (style) layer of review is the first one that can move to machines. But the A/B result shows that adding an AI commenter does not by itself make review faster.

#### A6. LLM-driven code migrations (Jan 2025)
- Cases: int32→int64 IDs in Ads, JUnit3→4, Joda→java.time, and stale flag cleanup. int32: 80% of modifications in landed CLs were fully AI-authored, with about a 50% reduction in total time. JUnit: about 87% of AI code was committed unchanged, covering 5,359 files and more than 149k lines in 3 months. Joda: about 89% time saved for small clusters. Validation loop: build plus unit tests, failures fed back to the model, and the best of several candidates kept by score. Landing: an expert checks the change and then shards it to owners. JUnit used the LSC system to split CLs for owner review, and **"review speed was the bottleneck."** Quotes: "Humans review the LLM-generated code the same way as any other code"; "Code reviews and change rollouts still require a human operator." https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972 [read]

#### A7. Agentic TF→JAX migration (2026)
- The system has a Planner (deterministic static analysis builds a dependency tree, then migration runs leaf-first), an Orchestrator (chunks the plan, injects playbooks, recovers from failures), and a Coder (a ReAct agent with a build/test fix loop). A blind Gemini 3 Pro "LLM Judge" scores the output against an architectural checklist. The process finishes with "expert human review." Speedups are 6.4x to 8x: one model went from about 16 hours to about 2 hours, another from about 32 hours to about 5 hours. Numerical-equivalence tests were not the primary metric. Agents "generate and run their own equivalency tests." Many migrated models are "not yet deployed." https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/developers-practitioners/6x-faster-migration-from-tensorflow-to-jax (2026-05-06) [read]; https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27296 [read]
- Pichai's "six times faster" migration claim at Next '26 refers to this. https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/infrastructure-and-cloud/google-cloud/cloud-next-2026-sundar-pichai/ (2026-04-22) [read]

### B. Meta

#### B1. Diff Risk Score: the un-freeze paper (TOSEM, Aug 2025)
- Before risk models, Meta froze changes in "red zone" periods, with tooling that blocked all diffs unless an exception was granted. Engineers objected because diffs to internal tools could not land even though they posed no user risk. Next came **ExpertPaths**: area experts listed risky paths by regex. Diffs touching those paths were blocked (red) or gated (yellow), and a gated diff needed "a rationale and potentially ... an approval from the area expert." In historical analysis, ExpertPaths gated 27.3% of diffs and captured 38% of SEVs. [read]
- The FileModel captures 56% of SEVs and the DiffModel captures 60.8% at the comparable gating level. The DiffModel formula is `DiffRisk ∼ had_sev + call_centrality + num_of_files + diff_only_creates_files + num_reviewers`. Thresholds are percentiles: the model is run over 3–6 months of diffs, and the gate is set so the desired fraction (for example 20%) exceeds it. [read]
- Production: in July, the FileModel gated 26% of diffs. 8.8% of all diffs (33% of gated diffs) landed after the developer gave a rationale. "No major SEVs" were introduced despite record traffic. Over winter 2023 the DiffModel gated 16% and 5.7% landed with a rationale. Compared with the prior year, landed diffs rose 42% and SEVs fell 52%, and SEV operating cost fell 47%. The model can "un-freeze 2 and 2.5 times more changes." https://mockus.us/papers/UnfreezeMeta.pdf (ACM TOSEM 34(7) Art. 211, 2025-08) [read]
- Mechanics worth copying [inference]: (1) the gate is a **percentile knob**, not a fixed probability; (2) the override is a **structured, logged rationale** and not a human approval; (3) the threshold changes by context (weekend 5%, yellow 10%, red 50%).

#### B2. DRS with LLMs (arXiv 2410.06351, Oct 2024)
- Rationale: "at a certain scale, it becomes impossible for a release engineering team to determine what should be released." Responsibility "appropriately and necessarily falls back on the engineer writing and reviewing the code." Teams get "the autonomy – and responsibility – to make the call." [read]
- What happens to a gated diff: it "will be blocked from landing." The engineer can wait for the freeze to end, modify the diff to lower its risk, or escalate with a standard reason to managers, who may approve. Phabricator shows the score, the reasons, a feedback control, and suggested actions. [read]
- Test set: 181,052 diffs and 305 SEVs. Top-5/10/50% gating captures 18.7/27.9/84.6% of SEVs with logistic regression (the production model, "for more than 9 months"), 26.2/42.3/88.5% with risk-aligned iDiffLlama-13B, and 23.6/35.7/83.0% with risk-aligned iCodeLlama-34B. StarBERT does worse than logistic regression. LLM inputs are the diff title, the test plan, and the unidiff. The LLM results are offline/historical. https://arxiv.org/html/2410.06351 [read]

#### B3. DRS today (Meta Engineering blog, 2025-08-06)
- Described as a fine-tuned Llama that predicts SEV likelihood and highlights risky snippets. A Risk Awareness Platform exposes APIs. "10,000+ code changes" landed during a major 2024 partner event that "previously could not have landed during a freeze," "with minimal production impact" (no incident counts given). There are 19 use cases: **build/test selection, code reviewer selection, release risk analysis**, and reliability. Planned: risk for config changes, **agents that generate risk-mitigating changes**, and natural-language explanations. Code changes cause "the plurality of SEVs at Meta." https://engineering.fb.com/2025/08/06/developer-tools/diff-risk-score-drs-ai-risk-aware-software-development-meta/ [read]
- Forge implication [inference]: one risk score drives several decisions at once: which tests to run, who reviews, whether the change can land now, and how it is released. That is the "policy over evidence" architecture, with the risk score as a key piece of evidence.

#### B4. MetaMateCR: AI fixes to review comments (arXiv 2507.13499)
- Llama 3.1 70B, fine-tuned on 64K ⟨comment, patch⟩ pairs (18K human-labeled, 46K classifier-labeled). Exact match offline: GPT-4o 59.22%, SmallLSFT 63.11%, LargeLSFT 67.96%. Production ActionableToApplied rose from 10.71% (GPT-4o) to 19.75% (LargeLSFT). ShownToApplied went from about 20% to 28.74%. [read]
- **Safety trial:** when reviewers saw AI patches, TimeInReview rose 5.5% (p=.029), and Meta rolled it back. Showing patches to authors only, and collapsing them for reviewers, removed the regression (p=.86). The authors question whether "reviewers should take on the responsibility of fixing authors' code." Volume is "thousands" of review comments per week. https://arxiv.org/html/2507.13499 [read]

#### B5. TestGen-LLM (FSE 2024)
- Filters: the test builds, passes on the first run, passes all five runs (anything less counts as flaky), and increases coverage. Each survivor is proposed as its own diff for human review. Of generated tests, 75% built, 57% passed reliably, and 25% increased coverage. 73% of recommendations were accepted. In the Facebook test-a-thon, 144 of 280 diffs were accepted. "It submits, for human review, only test cases that it can guarantee improve on the existing code base." https://arxiv.org/html/2402.09171 [read]

#### B6. JiTTests: just-in-time catching tests (2026)
- Tests are generated per diff when it is submitted and are not committed to the codebase. The pipeline infers the diff's intent, creates mutants, generates tests meant to catch the injected faults, filters with ensembles of rule-based and LLM assessors, and then reports to engineers. "Agentic development dramatically increases the pace of code change." Human review is needed "only ... when a bug is actually caught." https://engineering.fb.com/2026/02/11/developer-tools/the-death-of-traditional-testing-agentic-development-jit-testing-revival/ [read]
- Paper: 22,126 generated tests. Change-aware generation is 4x better than hardening tests and 20x better than coincidentally failing tests. Assessors cut human review load by 70%. 41 catches were reported, 8 were true positives, and 4 would have been serious. Human-accepted changes had more false positives and human-rejected changes had more true positives. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22832 (2026-01-30) [read]
- Forge implication [inference]: this is generated evidence attached to a change, which is the "proof" object in the brief's terms. But precision is low (about 20% of catches reported to humans are real), so the forge has to make a false alarm cheap to dismiss.

#### B7. CodeCompose and AI coding targets
- CodeCompose (InCoder-based) had 16K users and accounted for 8% of their code, with 40–58% hidden-line reproduction. https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12050 [read]
- Targets from leaked docs (BI via The Week, 2026-03-27): in the Creation org, 65% of engineers should have AI write more than 75% of their committed code (H1 2026). The Scalable ML org has a 50–80% AI-assisted goal that Meta says is "not tracked by metrics." In Q4 2025, the goal was 80% of mid-level and senior engineers adopting DevMate, Metamate, or Gemini. https://www.theweek.in/news/sci-tech/2026/03/27/... [read]. A late-2025 target of "55% of code changes ... agent-assisted" is [secondary].

### E. Uber

- uReview (2025-08-12). Pipeline: ingest (skip config, generated, and experimental files, add surrounding context) → three generators (Standard bugs, Best Practices, AppSec) → **a grader prompt scores quality and confidence, with thresholds per assistant, language, and category** → semantic dedupe → a category classifier suppresses categories with low historical value → inline comments with Useful/Not Useful buttons. The best configuration was Claude-4-Sonnet as generator and o4-mini-high as grader. It analyzes "over 90% of the weekly ~65,000 diffs" (also given as "65,000 per month" elsewhere in the post). It runs in CI across 6 monorepos with a median latency of 4 minutes. 75% of rated comments are useful, more than 65% of comments are addressed, and only 51% of human comments are addressed. Uber estimates about 1,500 hours per week saved, "nearly 39 developer years annually," assuming 10 minutes per commit for a second reviewer. Style and readability comments were rated poorly and removed, and linters are preferred for syntax. Uber built this in-house because it uses Phabricator. uReview "cannot assess overall correctness or system design." https://www.uber.com/blog/ureview/ [read]

## Pain points for agentic-scale dev (as seen in these orgs)

1. **Review throughput is the bottleneck.** Google's LLM migration paper names review speed as the limit [read]. Rosie had to invent global approvers because per-owner review did not scale [read]. Pichai's 75% figure means human reviewers now approve roughly three AI-written lines for each human-written one [inference from read].
2. **AI output in front of reviewers costs reviewer time.** In MetaMateCR, reviewers spent 5.5% more time when they saw AI patches [read]. AutoCommenter's A/B test showed no change in review duration [read]. Google 2024 notes authors "increasingly become a reviewer" [read].
3. **Precision limits everything.** Google edits are tuned to 50% precision [read]. AutoCommenter's useful ratio is 54–66% against an 80% target [read]. JiTTests catches are about 20% true positives [read]. uReview needed a separate grader and category suppression [read]. Any bot at scale must manage its false-positive budget.
4. **Blanket freezes and blanket gates break down.** Meta engineers objected to red-zone blocks on internal-tool diffs [read]. Release engineering "does not scale" [read].
5. **Provenance-based rules are hard to define and enforce.** Amazon's "AI-assisted" sign-off rule was disputed by Amazon itself within days [secondary]. Once 50–75% of code is AI-assisted, a rule keyed on "AI-assisted" covers most changes [inference].
6. **Blast radius matters more than authorship.** Amazon's reset is keyed to Tier-1 systems [secondary]. Meta's DRS features are dominated by prior SEVs, centrality, and core-service membership [read].
7. **Owners rubber-stamp at volume.** The SWE book admits owners give LSCs cursory review because "they trust the engineers generating LSCs too much" [read]. Formal human approval at volume can turn into a rubber stamp [inference].

## How orgs are solving them

| Pattern | Who | Mechanism | Human role kept |
|---|---|---|---|
| Author-side AI fixes | Google ML edits, Meta MetaMateCR, Microsoft "apply change" | Suggestion attached to a comment; author applies it | Author decides; reviewer still approves |
| AI as extra reviewer | Microsoft (>90% of PRs), Uber uReview (>90% of diffs), Google AutoCommenter | Inline comments, graded and filtered, with feedback loop | Human approver unchanged |
| Risk-routed landing | Meta DRS | Percentile gate; block, wait, de-risk, rationale, or manager escalation; also drives test selection and reviewer selection | Escalation to managers; rationale logged |
| Class-level approval | Google Rosie/LSC | Committee approves the LSC; global approver pattern-matches shards; anomalies get manual review | One expert per change class |
| Verified generation | Meta TestGen-LLM, Google migrations, Amazon Q transform | Build, test, flake, and coverage filters before any human sees it | Human accepts per diff (73% / 79% / 87% unchanged) |
| Evidence generated per change | Meta JiTTests | Generated catching tests run per diff; assessors filter | Human only on catch |
| Tightened human gate | Amazon Tier-1 reset | Extra approvals, documentation, controlled friction for 90 days | More senior human approval |

## Implications for a future forge

1. **Approval should be a typed predicate evaluated by policy, as in Google's LGTM/Owner/Readability split.** Each predicate (correctness, ownership, style, risk, test evidence) can be satisfied by a human, a checker, or an agent, and the policy sets which by path and risk band. Google already lets one human satisfy all three; a forge should also let a checker satisfy readability. AutoCommenter shows that is the layer that can be automated first. [inference]
2. **Ship a first-class risk score that drives routing, not just a label.** Meta's DRS sets the gate percentile, test selection, reviewer selection, and release risk. A forge should calculate a per-change risk score from history: prior incidents in the touched files, centrality, number of files, and whether the change only creates files. It should expose this as a policy input with a context knob (normal, weekend, freeze). [inference from read]
3. **Overrides should be a logged rationale, not a blocking human.** In Meta's production runs, 33% of gated diffs landed with a rationale and SEVs still fell. That is a practical middle ground between "named human approval" and "no review." [inference from read]
4. **Approval by change class (the Rosie pattern) fits agent swarms.** A human approves the *recipe* (prompt, playbook, tool, and the pattern of diffs it produces). The forge checks that each shard matches the pattern and sends only anomalies to people. The brief's "proof bar per path" should add a "proof bar per generator/recipe." [inference]
5. **Do not show AI output to reviewers by default.** Meta's A/B test is direct evidence that reviewer-facing AI patches slow review. Send AI fixes to the author or agent, and show reviewers only the evidence summary and anomalies. [inference from read]
6. **Generated evidence needs triage for its own false positives.** JiTTests and uReview both needed an assessor or grader layer. A forge "proof" system should include assessor ensembles and track how much reviewer effort each evidence source costs. [inference]
7. **Key policy on blast radius and criticality, not on authorship.** Amazon's provenance rule was disputed and becomes meaningless once most code is AI-assisted. Tier-1 criticality is the durable key. The brief's example ("a billing change needs an end-to-end run and a named human approval") matches Amazon's Tier-1 controls and Meta's red-zone paths. [inference]
8. **Context-dependent strictness (freeze modes) is a real requirement.** Meta's thresholds vary by event: weekend 5%, yellow 10%, red 50%. Amazon applied a 90-day "controlled friction" mode. The forge needs time-scoped policy overlays. [inference from read]
9. **Correction to the brief's framing:** the brief says the no-review model is "proven at 20 to 30 people." Big Tech evidence says something narrower. At scale, **review is reduced selectively, not removed**: by change class (Rosie), by risk percentile (DRS), and by evidence (JiTTests reviewed only on a catch). Every public statement still says the code is "approved by engineers." A forge for large organizations should treat the human approver as a resource that policy spends where risk is concentrated, not as something to remove. [inference]

## Contradictions and pushback

- **Google's "approved by engineers" at 75% AI-generated.** Either review load has risen sharply or approval has become shallower. Rosie shows Google has already accepted cursory owner review for a class of changes. There is no public Google data on review time per CL since 2024. [inference]
- **AI review gives mixed productivity results.** Microsoft reports 10–20% faster PR completion [read]. Google's AutoCommenter A/B test shows no significant change in review time [read]. Meta's reviewer-facing patches made review 5.5% slower [read]. Uber's 1,500 hours per week is a modeled estimate [read]. Google's "hundreds of thousands of hours" is a projection [read].
- **Meta's DRS headline numbers mix models.** The strong production outcomes (42% more diffs, 52% fewer SEVs) are for the *logistic regression* DiffModel in winter 2023. The LLM numbers are offline. The 2025 blog calls DRS a fine-tuned Llama and gives no metrics. Claims that "LLM risk gating cut SEVs 52%" would be wrong. Also, the year-over-year SEV comparison is observational, and traffic or other changes may confound it. [read + inference]
- **Amazon's position contradicts the press reports.** The FT and Ars reported senior sign-off on AI-assisted changes. Amazon denies it is universal and says only one incident involved AI tools, and that one was user error. The primary FT text and the Treadwell memo were not available (BI, CNBC, FT, and Ars were blocked or paywalled). [secondary]
- **Uber's diff-volume figure appears twice in the post with different units** (weekly versus monthly ~65k). [read]
- **TestGen-LLM's class-improvement figures are inconsistent** (11.5% in the abstract versus 196/1,979 ≈ 10% in Section 4). [read]

## Open questions

2. Has Meta moved DRS gating outside freezes, so it routes ordinary diffs to more or fewer reviewers or to auto-land? The blog lists "code reviewer selection" but gives no mechanics.
3. Did Amazon's 90-day Tier-1 reset (which ran to about June 2026) become permanent policy? What were the "deterministic and agentic safeguards" mentioned as the long-term replacement? [secondary hint]
4. Does any Big Tech forge let an AI reviewer's approval count toward the required approval, even for narrow classes such as tests-only diffs or generated migrations? I found no public evidence that any does.
